# Yellow Jacket Tour — The Narrative Rulebook

## A Round Played in Honey

Imagine a single hand of heads-up Texas Hold'em laid across a single hole of golf. The two cards in your sleeve are the lie of your ball off the tee. The flop is the fairway you find yourself on. The turn is the approach. The river is the green. And at the end, you score the hole — not by counting chips, but by the shape of your hand.

A royal flush is a hole-in-one. A full house, depending on its kicker, is an eagle. A flush or a straight is a birdie. One pair, depending on whether it's pocket tens or a low pair on a busted board, is the difference between par and bogey. High card, with nothing to back it, is a double bogey. Hand strength becomes ground gained or lost on the scorecard, and lower wins — exactly as in golf.

Across eighteen holes you accumulate a number, the way a Masters competitor accumulates one across Augusta National. Across four such rounds — seventy-two holes — you accumulate a championship total. The yellow jacket goes to the lowest number on Sunday afternoon.

## Honey Is the Stake, Not the Stack

Beside the scorecard runs a second ledger: honey. Every hole opens with honey already in the pot — two on the front nine, four on the back, the way a back-nine Sunday at the Masters carries more weight than a Thursday opener. From there, every bet, every raise, every call modifies a single number: the agreed total of the hole's pot.

This is unlike poker. In poker you contribute chips into a growing pile and the pile is the sum of everyone's contributions. In Yellow Jacket, when you raise, you do not add to the pot — you propose what the pot now is. When your opponent calls, they do not match your contribution — they accept your proposal. The pot is always exactly the most recently agreed total, and never anything else. Closer in spirit to an English auction than to a poker pot, with each successive bid setting a new ceiling that the other player either accepts or walks away from.

Honey transfers at hole resolution: the winner of the hand takes the agreed total as a credit. At round's end, your net honey across all eighteen holes is divided by nine and subtracted from your scorecard total. (For a seventy-two-hole final the divisor is thirty-six. The ratio is the same; the format absorbs the difference.) The result is the round score that decides the round.

This is why a Major's stroke cap is set at nine: a max-pot Major win contributes exactly one stroke to the scorecard. Self-similar at every tier.

## When You Lose, You Lose Honourably

What happens at showdown when the cards are turned and your hand is the weaker one? Yellow Jacket has two answers, and the variant you're playing decides between them.

Under the **competitive Yellow Jacket variant**, the loser of a decisive showdown posts a fixed bogey — one stroke. The fold rule and the loser rule are identical. Any non-winning resolution costs the same single stroke, regardless of how strong the losing hand was. Skill expression sharpens; champion totals drift above par into the +12 to +28 range, the way WSOP Main Event chip counts rarely tell a Masters-realistic story.

Under the **casual Bumblebee variant**, the loser scores their own hand's golf value. A flush that lost to a full house still records a birdie. The texture is gentler; champion totals drift below par into the −10 to −18 range, in line with what a real PGA major champion shoots over four rounds.

A small probability-calibrated overlay sits on top. Under the competitive variant, a loser whose hand is a straight or better is honoured with their own hand's score rather than the flat bogey — a respected loss. And in the rare frame where both players show full house or better with at most a one-category gap between them, the winner takes an additional stroke off — a cooler. Set-over-set, full-over-full, quads-over-quads. Less than one in a thousand showdowns. When it fires, it's lore.

## Folding Is a Concession, Not a Reveal

When a player folds, the cards never turn. The non-folder takes par on the scorecard and the previously agreed pot in honey. The folder takes a bogey and forfeits that same previously agreed pot. Important: only what was already agreed transfers — the unaccepted proposal that triggered the fold is moot.

Mechanically, this is identical to a conceded hole in match-play golf. You give up the hole; you take a bogey for the trouble; the agreed wager passes; you walk to the next tee.

Strategically, the consequence cuts hard. With a strong hand, forcing a fold caps your reward at par plus the previously agreed honey. The minus-three or minus-four golf score your hand would have realised at showdown is gone. So premium hands prefer bets that get called rather than bets that fold. With a weak hand, the fold is a cheap exit if the agreed pot is small, and an expensive one if late-street wagering has stacked it. The whole rhythm of pressure runs the wrong way from poker: bet for value, not for fold equity.

## The Tied-Hole Carry

Sometimes the cards do not decide the hole — both players hold the same kind of hand and the kickers do not break the tie. In that case both players post par, the hole is resolved without a winner, and the agreed pot rolls forward to the next hole as starting honey on top of the mandatory opener. Multiple consecutive ties stack: a chain of three tied holes can stand the next decisive hole's opener up at twelve, fifteen, twenty honey. Whoever wins that next decisive hole sweeps the entire stack at once.

This is the closest the game comes to a poker side-pot — except it carries forward through holes rather than splitting between players. Like carryover money in a Friday-night skins game, it turns the next decisive hand into the most consequential one of the round.

## The Stroke Cap

Each event tier sets a maximum agreed pot per hole — the stroke cap. Regular events cap at six honey, Majors at nine, Main Event early matches at eight, the Main Event 72-hole finals at eighteen. The Major cap of nine is the anchor — a max-pot Major win contributes exactly one stroke to the round score after divisor normalisation. The Main finals cap of eighteen is twice the anchor: at the divisor of thirty-six that governs a 72-hole final, a max-pot Main-finals win contributes half a stroke. The long format absorbs the higher cap.

Within each hole, the cap unlocks progressively across the four streets. Twenty-five percent is available by Tea Box (preflop), fifty by Drive (flop), seventy-five by Hazard (turn), the full hundred by Putt (river). Streets unlock new tiers of the bet vocabulary: a Tea Box typically only allows the smallest sizes (probe, press); a Drive opens the pot-sized bet; a Hazard opens the polarising raise; a Putt opens the shove. The decision space genuinely widens as the hand develops, exactly as in no-limit Hold'em where the deepest pots are reached only when both players have committed their way through earlier streets.

## The Tournament Around the Hand

A Tour event is four eighteen-hole rounds. Seventy-two holes total. After each round, two elimination mechanics tighten the field.

The first is the **survival cushion**: any player whose cumulative score exceeds the round leader's by more than the cushion is eliminated. The cushion runs +12 strokes after Round 1, +8 after Round 2, +5 after Round 3. Round 4's cushion is moot — the championship round resolves directly by score. The cushion produces the missed-cut moment: a player twelve strokes off the lead at the end of Thursday is finished, the way a PGA pro who shoots 82 on day one knows the weekend is gone.

The second is the **cut**: the bottom fraction of the surviving field is removed at each round boundary. No cut after Round 1, fifty percent after Round 2 (the primary cut at hole 36), fifty percent again after Round 3 (the secondary cut at hole 54). This is the standard PGA Tour weekend-cut structure, transplanted directly. Cushion fires before cut at each boundary, so a player too far from the lead is cushion-eliminated before they can occupy a cut spot.

Eliminated players freeze. Their scorecard ends where they left it; they skip every subsequent round. Their final position is decided first by elimination depth (later survival ranks higher) and within an elimination tier, by score. Making the cut always beats missing it — a player who survives Round 3 with a worse score still finishes ahead of a Round-2 elimination who had a better score.

The top fifteen percent of finish positions cash, with payouts decaying geometrically across the cash line. The deepest run earns the largest single share.

## Multi-Way Tables

The default engine is heads-up. The multi-way variant seats six or nine players at one table for the first three rounds, then collapses to a heads-up final between the top two survivors for the championship round. At a six- or nine-player table the wagering reverts to poker-native matched contributions — every player puts the same contribution in, the pot is the sum, exactly as in cash-game Hold'em. The agreed-total semantics return for the heads-up final.

Per-player honey caps scale down with table size so that per-pair stroke variance stays comparable to heads-up. At table sizes above six the mandatory opener doubles, since per-seat equity thins and the opener has to compensate. The casual variant becomes the default at multi-way — eight or nine simultaneous fixed bogeys per pot, under the competitive rule, would drag champion scores too far above par. The casual rule lets a strong losing hand still post its own score, which keeps multi-way totals golf-realistic.

## Two Currencies, Two Roles

The Tour runs on two denominations and confusing them is the most common newcomer error.

**Nectar** is the bankroll. Functionally, dollars. It pays event buy-ins, it sits in your wallet between sessions, it's what cash-table chips convert to one-for-one, it's what prize payouts arrive as. Single ledger, persistent.

**Honey** is the per-event wager unit. It exists only inside the boundary of a tour event. Honey is what gets bet and raised and called, what the stroke caps are denominated in, what gets divided by the round-length divisor to produce the credit on the scorecard. Honey is not a balance you hold — it's an internal abstraction that lets per-hand wagering be capped without ever touching your real bankroll. When the event ends, the only thing that crosses the boundary is the Nectar prize.

Real tournament chips work the same way. A WSOP chip stack is not redeemable; only the final cash payout is real money. Yellow Jacket makes that distinction explicit by giving the two units different names.

## The Economy

Direct buy-ins by tier: Regular $100, Major $1,000, Main $10,000. A satellite ladder runs alongside — a $15 buy-in at Tier 1 wins a Regular seat, $150 at Tier 2 wins a Major seat, a four-rung Tier 3 ladder ($500, $1,500, $4,000, $5,000) wins a Main seat. The progressive requirement: cash in a lower tier to unlock the next satellite tier.

Rake is tiered. The Tour foundation takes 1.5% of every Regular buy-in, 2.0% of every Major buy-in, 3.0% of every Main buy-in. Real WSOP Main rake runs around 6%; real PGA Tour rake is effectively zero (sponsor-funded). The 1.5/2.0/3.0 split is the calibrated middle that lets elite skill expression produce neutral-to-positive ROI on Regulars and Majors while the marquee Main funds the elite tier's career upside.

Sponsor purses, when configured, ride on top of the prize pool and are not raked. Modeled directly after PGA Tour broadcast money: the more sponsorship a season attracts, the better the player ROI, with no offsetting house cut.

The top fifteen percent of any field cash. Geometric decay across positions: each successive in-the-money finish pays the prior position multiplied by 0.74. The champion takes the largest single share; the cut line takes the smallest.

## Why It Reads the Way It Does

Yellow Jacket sits in a precise spot between two existing competitive forms. It is poker by hand, golf by score, agreed-total by wager. Each lineage contributes something the other cannot.

From poker comes the per-hand decision space — five named streets, equity ranges, position, the bluff, the cooler. From golf comes the cumulative scorecard — par as a reference line, the seventy-two-hole format that absorbs single-hand variance, the cut as the structural elimination point, the green jacket on Sunday afternoon. From the auction lineage comes agreed-total wagering — the ceiling that the opponent either accepts or steps away from, instead of the matched chip stack of cash poker.

The result is a game where the per-hand drama of no-limit Hold'em is preserved exactly, but the tournament arc resembles a four-round PGA major rather than a chip-and-blind WSOP grind. A bad hand on Thursday does not bust you; it costs you strokes you have to recover before Friday's cut. A great Sunday-back-nine hand at maximum pot moves you exactly one stroke up the leaderboard. The Yellow Jacket is the green jacket, traced in honey.
